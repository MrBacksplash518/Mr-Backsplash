Estimate conversation guide

What photos & details help David?

A useful first message shows the whole space, the difficult details, what you want to change, and where the project is located. This guide makes that quick to assemble.

A close-up can show a crack but hide the room. A wide photo can show the room but hide the joint. The most useful set moves from context to detail, then adds a short written description.

Use a simple wide-to-close photo sequence

  1. Start at the doorway. Photograph the full room or wall so the location and access are clear.
  2. Turn to each side. Capture every wall, floor boundary, cabinet run, shower wall, or connected space involved in the project.
  3. Move closer to the work area. Show the full backsplash field, shower, floor, or repair area without cropping off its edges.
  4. Finish with details. Photograph outlets, corners, windows, drains, plumbing penetrations, thresholds, damaged joints, loose tile, and open edges.
  5. Include scale. Add approximate dimensions or place a tape measure in a separate photo without covering the condition you want David to see.

Keep the camera level when possible and make sure the detail is in focus. Daylight plus the room lights often shows plane and color more clearly than a flash close-up.

For damage

Send at least one close photo and one wider photo that shows where the damage sits in the full installation.

Add approximate dimensions and material information

Early measurements establish scale; they are not a substitute for the measurements used to plan cuts and quantities. Label what you measured, such as wall length, counter-to-cabinet height, room width and length, shower opening, or the size of a damaged tile.

If you have selected a product, photograph the face and the box label or send a product link. Include tile or plank size, color, finish, and any trim, accent, grout, or transition piece. If you have not selected material, send inspiration as a preference, not as proof that the same layout fits your room.

  • Show multiple pieces when color or pattern varies.
  • Photograph spare matching tile or leftover packaging for a repair.
  • Say whether the material is purchased, ordered, sampled, or still being compared.
  • Keep manufacturer information available for use and installation requirements.

Write the facts that a photo cannot communicate

A short list is better than a long story with the key details buried. Include:

  • Town or ZIP: enough to establish where the project is located.
  • Type of work: backsplash, bathroom or shower tile, floor tile, repair, regrouting, or laminate flooring.
  • Goal: what you want installed, changed, repaired, or understood.
  • Existing surface: what is there now if you know, without guessing at hidden layers.
  • Related changes: cabinets, counters, plumbing, fixtures, shower glass, paint, trim, appliances, or adjoining floors.
  • Decisions still open: tile, pattern, grout, edge, layout, or material availability.

If access is unusual, mention stairs, parking limits, building entry, occupied areas, or fixed items that cannot be moved. Avoid sending sensitive documents or unrelated personal information in the photo background.

For a repair, include the condition’s history

Say when you first noticed the issue, whether it is changing, and whether the same area has been repaired before. Mention movement, moisture, staining, soft material, odor, a known leak, or a tile that shifts. Do not clean away loose material or cover the area before documenting it.

If water is actively leaking or building material is wet or soft, state that at the beginning of the message. Surface photos help triage the conversation, but urgent water or plumbing issues may need prompt attention beyond a finish repair.

For a grout concern, show whether the condition is isolated, follows a corner, repeats across a floor, or sits beside a transition. For a broken tile, show the surrounding pieces and any matching spare.

Know what photos cannot confirm

Photos cannot reveal every substrate layer, bond condition, moisture path, floor movement, wall flatness, or hidden obstruction. They are a strong starting point for deciding what questions to ask and whether the project appears to fit the service, but they do not turn hidden conditions into known facts.

David personally handles the estimate conversation and installation. After reviewing the message, he can tell you what additional views, product details, measurements, or on-site review would be useful.

A complete first text

“Hi David, I’m in [town or ZIP] and I’m planning [type of work]. The area is approximately [dimensions]. I want to [goal]. The existing condition is [what you know]. I can attach wide room photos, close details, and [material or spare tile].”

Photo and estimate questions

Can I send a short video?

Yes. A slow, steady video can add context, but include still photos too so David can inspect specific details without searching through the clip.

What if I do not have measurements yet?

Start with clear photos and say that measurements are not available. David can tell you which approximate dimensions would make the next conversation more useful.

Will photos be enough for an estimate?

They may be enough to begin discussing the work, but the answer depends on the service and what the images reveal. Hidden conditions, final measurements, or access may require more information or an on-site look.
